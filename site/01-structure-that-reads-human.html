Why most AI cover letters fail

They fail before the second sentence, because they commit the three tells:

  • The tell of vague enthusiasm. "I am excited to apply for this exciting opportunity at your exciting company." Three "excités" in one breath, and the reader learns nothing.
  • The tell of the AI voice. "I am confident that my unique blend of skills perfectly aligns with your requirements." Nobody talks like that. Nobody.
  • The tell of the resume re-run. The letter just repeats the resume in paragraph form. A letter that only summarizes what the reader already has in front of them earns zero extra information.

A letter that reads human does the opposite of all three: it names something specific about the employer, it speaks in ordinary sentences, and it makes exactly one argument.

The one-argument principle

Every good cover letter makes one argument. Not five. The argument is almost always one of these:

  1. "I already do parts of this job." (Same skills, adjacent context.)
  2. "I've made this exact switch before." (Pivot credibility.)
  3. "I've been a customer/patient/user of the thing you do." (Lived understanding.)
  4. "Here's the specific thing I'd fix here, and why I'm the one to fix it." (Insight play — risky but memorable.)

Pick one for each letter. Build the whole letter around it. If you cannot pick, you have not read the posting carefully enough yet.

The structure (6 parts, under 250 words)

PartLengthJob
1. Greeting1 lineA real name, if you can find one. "Dear Hiring Team" beats "To Whom It May Concern" — but "Dear Ms. Patel" wins.
2. The hook1–2 sentencesName the role and one true, specific thing about you or the employer. Not flattery — specifics.
3. The proof paragraph3–4 sentencesYour one argument, backed by one concrete result or story from your actual history.
4. The fit line + bridge1–2 sentencesAcknowledge the gap in your background honestly, then bridge it. (Crucial for pivots.)
5. The forward look1–2 sentencesWhat you'd want to do in the first 90 days, if honest and knowable — else what draws you to this role specifically.
6. Sign-off1 line"Thank you for reading. — Sam" energy. Short.

That is the entire letter. Under 250 words total. Longer is not better; it is just longer.

What the hook should never be

  • "I am writing to apply for..." (the reader already knows — it's the cover letter slot)
  • "I hope this letter finds you well." (it finds you in a stack of 200)
  • Any sentence that could be pasted into a letter for a different job unchanged.

The hook is where AI helps most if you prompt it right: ask for five hook candidates, all from true facts, then edit the best one into your own voice. Chapter 3 gives you the exact prompts.

Reading level beats word count

Short sentences. Ordinary words. One paragraph idea each. If a sentence needs a re-read, it will not get one — it will get a skip. A hiring manager spends about 20 seconds scanning a cover letter. Structure is what gets you 20 more.

Checklist before you send

  • One argument, chosen and consistent
  • Hook names something specific and true
  • Proof paragraph contains at least one number, project, or event
  • Any gap acknowledged honestly and bridged briefly
  • Under 250 words
  • Read it aloud — if you stumble reading it, rewrite that sentence
  • No sentence could survive a swap into any other application unchanged
